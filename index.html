1. Psychology is a science

Psychology is the scientific study of behavior and mental processes. It includes much more than therapy and psychological disorders.

  • Behavior: observable actions, such as speaking, studying, or helping.
  • Mental processes: thoughts, memories, emotions, perceptions, and decisions.
  • Empirical evidence: information obtained through systematic observation and measurement.
  • Critical thinking: evaluating evidence, questioning assumptions, and considering alternative explanations.
Test clue: A scientific explanation relies on evidence. “I believe it” or “I know someone who experienced it” does not, by itself, establish a scientific conclusion.

Try it: A friend says a study strategy works because it helped one person. What additional information would you want? Consider systematic comparisons, measurement, and alternative explanations.

Distinctions to practice

  • Social vs. personality: influence from other people vs. relatively stable individual traits.
  • Developmental vs. educational: lifespan change vs. teaching and learning.
  • Health vs. clinical: physical wellness and illness vs. psychological assessment and treatment.
  • Evolutionary vs. behavioral genetics: possible ancestral adaptive functions vs. genetic and environmental contributions to differences.
The bank treats cognitive psychology as a subspecialty of experimental psychology. Psychologists across specialties can conduct experiments; experiments are not restricted to experimental psychologists.

One behavior, five explanations

A student avoids giving presentations.

  • Neuroscience: What biological arousal accompanies the anxiety?
  • Psychodynamic: Are unconscious conflicts contributing?
  • Behavioral: Has escaping presentations reinforced avoidance?
  • Cognitive: Does the student predict embarrassment?
  • Humanistic: How can the student exercise choice and pursue growth?
Match the explanation, not just the behavior. A question about memory suggests cognitive psychology; a question explicitly about the neural activity supporting memory emphasizes neuroscience.

5. Major debates

DebateDistinction
Nature vs. nurtureInherited/biological influences vs. experience/environmental influences
Conscious vs. unconscious causesAware thoughts and intentions vs. processes outside awareness
Observable behavior vs. internal mental processesDirectly measurable actions vs. thoughts, memories, and interpretations
Free will vs. determinismCapacity to choose vs. behavior shaped by prior causes and constraints
Individual differences vs. universal principlesWhat makes people distinctive vs. patterns shared across people
  • Parenting, schooling, and experiences point toward nurture.
  • Inherited biological tendencies point toward nature.
  • Watson's strong claims about training children emphasize nurture.
  • Humanism emphasizes free will and individual experience.
  • Negative self-talk suggests a cognitive explanation.
  • Hidden childhood conflicts suggest unconscious causes.

Contemporary explanations often combine biological, psychological, and social influences. Genetic influence does not make behavior inevitable.

6. Scientific method and testable ideas

Identify a question → formulate an explanation → carry out research → communicate findings.
  • Developing a hypothesis belongs to formulating an explanation.
  • Defining procedures, collecting data, and analyzing data belong to carrying out research.
  • Presenting findings at a conference belongs to communicating findings.
TermMeaningExample
TheoryBroad explanation organizing evidence and generating predictionsShared responsibility can reduce individual helping.
HypothesisSpecific, testable predictionPeople who believe others can help will offer help less often.
VariableA characteristic that can take different valuesNumber of bystanders; amount of helping
Operational definitionExact procedure for measuring or manipulating a variableHelping = pressing an assistance button within 30 seconds.
ResearchSystematic inquiry aimed at discovering knowledgeA planned study testing a prediction

Theory explains. Hypothesis predicts. Operational definition specifies the procedure.

“Happiness is feeling good” is abstract. “Happiness is a rating from 1 to 10 on this question” is operational. A concept can have multiple operational definitions.

Bystander effect and diffusion of responsibility

When several others are available to help, an individual may feel less personal responsibility to act. This is diffusion of responsibility. Distinguish having others present from believing others are able to help.

7. Research methods

Ask: What is the researcher actually doing?

MethodIdentifying actionStrength and limitation
Archival researchExamines existing recordsOften efficient; records may be incomplete or unsuitable.
Naturalistic observationRecords naturally occurring behavior without interveningStudies usual settings; offers limited control.
SurveyAsks people questionsCan reach many people; sampling and self-report biases matter.
Case studyIntensively examines one person or a small groupProvides depth; generalizability is limited.
Ethnographic researchStudies a culture in depth in its own settingProvides rich cultural context; time intensive and context dependent.
Correlational researchMeasures relationships between variablesSupports prediction; does not by itself establish causation.
ExperimentManipulates a variable and compares outcomes under controlled conditionsSupports causal inference when well designed; has practical and ethical limits.

Methods can overlap: surveys or archival records can supply data for a correlational study. Read whether the question asks about data collection or the overall research design.

Sample vs. population

  • Population: the entire group of interest.
  • Sample: the people actually studied.
  • Representativeness: how well the sample reflects the target population.
A sample is part of a population. Randomly choosing students from one class does not make them representative of all adults.

College students are often convenient research participants, but their education and other characteristics may differ from the general population.

8. Correlations: sign, strength, and limits

A correlation coefficient, r, ranges from −1.00 to +1.00.

  • Sign = direction.
  • Absolute value = strength of the linear relationship.
CoefficientInterpretation
+.70Higher values of one variable tend to accompany higher values of the other.
−.80Higher values of one tend to accompany lower values of the other; stronger than +.70.
+.05Very little linear association.
−1.00 or +1.00Perfect linear association.

Increasing strength: +.10 → +.50 → −.60.

Negative does not mean weak. Positive does not mean good. Ignore the sign when ranking strength.

Correlation does not establish causation

If study time and grades are associated, studying might influence grades, grades might influence study habits, or another variable might influence both.

A coefficient near zero indicates little linear association; a nonlinear relationship may still exist.

9. Experiments: identify each component

Example: A researcher randomly assigns students to a quiet room or a room with recorded conversation, then measures words recalled.

ComponentMeaningExample
Independent variable (IV)What the researcher manipulatesSound condition
Dependent variable (DV)Measured outcomeNumber of words recalled
Experimental conditionCondition of interestRecorded conversation
Control/comparison conditionBaseline for comparisonQuiet room
Random assignmentChance allocation to conditionsA random-number process assigns rooms.
ConfoundAnother factor systematically differs across conditionsOnly the conversation room also has brighter lighting.
IV = intervention varied. DV = data collected as the outcome. Ask: What changed? What was measured?

Random sampling vs. random assignment

  • Sampling: selecting people from a population.
  • Assignment: allocating participants to research conditions.

Random assignment reduces systematic group differences on average; it does not guarantee identical groups.

A control group alone does not establish causation. Manipulation, appropriate comparison, and control of alternative explanations work together. Experiments can also compare different conditions within the same participants.

10. Bias, placebos, and blinding

TermMeaning
Participant expectationsParticipants' beliefs about the study affect their behavior.
Experimenter expectationsResearchers' expectations influence interactions, measurement, or interpretation.
Experimental biasSystematic distortion of research results.
PlaceboAn inactive or sham comparison treatment.
Placebo effectTreatment expectations influence outcomes.
Double-blind procedureParticipants and relevant interacting or assessing researchers do not know condition assignments.

A placebo comparison helps assess effects beyond treatment expectations. Double blinding reduces both participant and experimenter expectation effects.

Assignment addresses group allocation. Blinding addresses knowledge of assignment.

11. Ethics and interpreting findings

Informed consent → participation → debriefing.
  • Informed consent: explains participation, relevant risks, voluntariness, and withdrawal rights.
  • Debriefing: explains the study afterward and addresses any deception.
  • Deception: sometimes serves a research purpose but requires ethical justification and oversight.
  • Confidentiality: protects participants' information.
  • Animal research: requires appropriate care and efforts to minimize pain, illness, and distress.

Consent is an ongoing process, not merely a signature.

TermMeaningAvoid this mistake
Statistical significanceA result meets a statistical criterion under a specified model.It does not prove a hypothesis or establish practical importance.
ReplicationA new test of a previous finding, sometimes with modified procedures or samples.It is not simply combining old study results.
Meta-analysisStatistically combines findings from multiple studies.It is not a single repeat experiment.
GeneralizabilityHow far findings apply beyond the studied people and conditions.A large convenience sample is not automatically representative.

In the bank, a “significant outcome” refers to statistical meaning. Statistical significance is different from clinical relevance, effect size, and certainty. Replication and converging evidence increase confidence.

12. Careers, diversity, and the field

Psychology majors develop skills in evaluating evidence, synthesizing information, research, and communication. A bachelor's degree does not automatically qualify someone for independent psychological practice.

PreparationChapter-level distinction
PhDOften emphasizes research alongside advanced training; some programs also train clinicians.
PsyDGenerally emphasizes clinical practice while including research training.
PsychiatristA physician, commonly with an MD or DO, specializing in mental health; may prescribe medication.

Clinical and counseling psychology overlap in actual practice. The bank's adjustment-versus-disorders contrast is a simplified chapter distinction.

Psychologists work in universities, healthcare, businesses, government, and other settings. Neuroscience connects many specialties.

Researcher and participant diversity helps broaden the questions asked and the populations to which conclusions apply. A narrow research sample can limit generalizability.

Verify exact workforce percentages, employment rankings, pay comparisons, and growth predictions against assigned course materials. These claims depend on the source and time period.
